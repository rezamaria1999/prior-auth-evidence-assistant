1Problem Brief · the problem

Every 100 denials. About 12 get appealed, and roughly 9 of those are overturned.

Appealing takes initiative, paperwork and usually an advocate. Whether a first decision ever gets checked depends on who the member is.

1Problem Brief · Humana's position, for context

Humana runs the highest prior authorization volume in Medicare Advantage at the lowest denial rate.

KFF notes that plans requiring prior auth on more services deny a smaller share of them. So the rate gap is mostly denominator, and per member the exposure matches. What matters here is the absolute number: 5.8% of roughly 16 million requests is about 930,000 denials a year.

Humana has also already removed 340+ codes from prior authorization. The easy approvals are gone. What is left is the hard residual.

1Problem Brief · who this is for

The clinician who reviews a case before a denial can be issued. CMS requires that person to exist.

Under CMS-4201-F, an adverse medical-necessity decision must be reviewed by "a physician or other appropriate health care professional with expertise in the field of medicine … before" the determination is issued.
  • This adds no step and no approver. It improves what is on the screen at a review that already happens and is already mandated.
  • The nurse reviewer benefits too. Cases arrive with the record already searched instead of as an unsorted packet.
  • The member never sees it, and that is the point. Whether their first decision is correct stops depending on whether they can appeal.
1Problem Brief · how big

Per 10,000 denials in one service line

Most denials are correct. The ones that are not are only found when somebody appeals, and the group that never appeals is roughly ten times the group that does.

OIG's 13% comes from a 2019 sample across 15 organizations, not from Humana, and Humana already denies below the industry rate. So treat 13% as the ceiling. Even at a third of it, this is tens of thousands of cases a year.

2Service Design · what went wrong

Nobody made a mistake. The evidence was in the building, in three places.

  • Eight paid therapy claims across 5.4 weeks, in Humana's own claims system. Humana paid for the therapy that proves the four-week trial happened.
  • A therapy discharge summary on page 9 of the 14-page fax: "0 of 3 functional goals met… symptoms have not responded to conservative management."
  • Meloxicam on the medication list since March, still active.
Day 7 counts as a success in every operating metric Humana runs. The clock was met and the letter went out on time. Margaret is also the lucky one, because her practice had staff who could file the appeal.

3Prototype · where the AI sits in the design

The criterion has two halves. Only one of them can be answered without reading.

LayerThe question it answersHow certain
1Claims historyDid Humana pay for therapy, and across how many weeks?CertainA paid claim is a transaction record
2Structured fieldsPrior imaging, red flags, active medicationsCertainDeterministic lookup
3Language model reads the narrativeDid the patient get better?A readingSo it must quote text we verify against the source

L34220 needs a four-week trial and that the patient did not respond to it. Layers 1 and 2 are certain, and they are silent on the second half — there is no field anywhere that records whether someone got better. That sits in prose, in a different document every time. Running the certain layers first is cheaper and narrows what the model has to find.

3Prototype · four things it is not allowed to do
  • It cannot deny. There is no code path from the model to an adverse determination. Not a setting anyone could change later, absent from the architecture.
  • It cannot quote something that is not there. Every quote is checked by exact string match against the source before display. Plain string comparison, no model involved. Failures are dropped.
  • It cannot settle a disagreement. When the claims layer and the narrative layer contradict each other, both appear, marked as conflicting. The clinician decides.
  • It cannot imply a denial by going quiet. The empty state reads "no supporting evidence identified in the submitted record. This is not a coverage recommendation."

4Product Roadmap · north star

First-decision accuracy

The share of first decisions that hold up on independent re-review — measured against the complete retrievable record, everything Humana can reach, not only the pages that happened to arrive with the request.

  • Measurable from phase 4. It needs the physician-adjudicated audit set, which is why that is funded in phase 0 rather than later.
  • Evidence completeness is the phase 1 proxy — how much of the retrievable support was actually in front of the reviewer.
  • If completeness climbs and accuracy does not follow, the premise is wrong and phase 3 should not happen.
4Product Roadmap · the cheapest test available

One number, available this quarter, before anything gets built.

Of the denials Humana overturned last quarter, what share turned on evidence already in Humana's possession when the first decision was made?

No audit set. No model. The appeal files already exist.

It is built on appeals, so it inherits the same bias as everything else here. That makes it a test of the premise rather than a performance metric — and it either supports building this or kills it in two weeks.

5Key Tradeoff · what I gave up

This system can only argue for approval. It has no way to argue for denial.

A tool that surfaced evidence in both directions would also help on cases that genuinely should be denied. That capability is given up deliberately, and giving it up is what makes the product safe to put in front of a clinician.

It also costs money. Most wrongly denied members never appeal, so today Humana does not pay for that care. Getting more of those first decisions right means paying for care Humana currently does not.
